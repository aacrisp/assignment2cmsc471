White Hat

For our white hat visualization, we decided to use the NYPD civilian allegations data set. Specifically, we segmented the data based on allegation category and year, and then calculated the outcome which we labeled as substantiation rate. We also included a marker in the form of an orange dot in order to indicate a low sample size. Furthermore, we included a drop down menu in order to switch between substantiation rate and total count of allegations, in order to allow for more clear analysis of the data. We decided to use a color scale based on the number of allegations or percent substantiated, and chose blue as it is typically a color associated with the police. For percent substantiated specifically, we noticed that it was never greater than 50%, so we chose that as the darkest blue, in order to make the other percentage differences more noticeable in terms of color, as it would be harder to tell if the scale went to 100%. We also decided to include a tooltip when a box is hovered over, in order to more clearly explain the data for that specific allegation in that specific year. We made these design choices in order to break down the data as much as possible, and allow viewers to understand the trends over time. We wanted to show how substantiation rates have generally risen, although not in every category, as well as how the total count of allegations has changed over time, which is why we included a drop down to switch between the two. In addition, by including the orange dots on both the substantiation and total count parts, we allow viewers to understand which pieces of data may be more reliable, and which ones to take with a grain of salt due to a lower sample size. Finally, the data set only includes officers who had at least one substantiated allegation, so the rates shown are higher than they would be for all complaints, and the 2019 counts are probably incomplete.

Black Hat

For our black hat visualization, we once again used the NYPD civilian allegations data set, but this time, we chose to display it in a different manner. We did not segment the data by year, and instead only separated by allegation, hiding how the number changes over time. In addition, we combined the "exonerated" and "unsubstantiated" categories into one new label - "no misconduct finding" - in order to distort the visualization and mislead the viewer into thinking that the allegations are overwhelmingly dismissed. This is misleading because the two outcomes mean different things: exonerated means the alleged conduct happened but did not violate NYPD rules, while unsubstantiated means there was not enough evidence, and about 27% of allegations were in fact substantiated. Furthermore, we chose to use red in order to alarm the viewer, and make them think that most misconduct complaints are dismissed, and combined that with a misleading and suggestive title to indicate that the NYPD is a corrupt organization. We also intentionally made the tooltip that is displayed on hover misleading and not specific - it displays "allegations in group" and "total allegations" without stating their actual outcome. In addition, we included percentages on both sides of the bar to highlight how much greater the no misconduct finding side was, and demonstrate that a very small percent of allegations are substantiated. Circling back to how we split the data, we chose to not split by year in order to hide the trends that can be seen in the white hat visualization. By only showing the total percentages, we can disguise the fact that the overall substantiation rate rose from 17.5% in 2010 to 30.9% in 2019. Instead, by including all the data together, there is no perception of any change over time. We also gave no hint of how the data was filtered: the data set only includes officers who had at least one substantiated allegation, so the percentages describe that group of officers rather than all NYPD complaints, and nothing on the chart tells the viewer that.

Extra Credit Features

  • Reveal the tricks. A button on the black hat chart redraws it as the "honest version" of the same data. The red "No Misconduct Finding" bar splits back into Exonerated and Unsubstantiated, each outcome gets its own color, and the headline, legend, and hover text change to match. A short list then explains each distortion, using numbers calculated from the data. This is particularly insightful as it lets the viewer see the same data change meaning when the framing changes.

Development

Given each member’s availability and skill sets, Jackson handled the majority of the programming for this assignment. Arun did the two write-ups on the White and Black Hat visualizations and deployed the site. MJ completed the Development and AI Usage write-ups, shot the demo video, did some code refactoring, and added the Extra Credit features. The development process was quite smooth, starting with deciding on the dataset. From there, Jackson ran some ideas back and forth to figure out the design of the two visualizations before settling on one design and checking with the other team members. The rest of the process involved manual adjustments, adding scaling, selecting better colors, dropdowns, and the tooltip. Additionally, other features were added for the black hat visualization such as the percentages added to the horizontally stacked bar chart. After this process, more code refactoring was done to clean up and simplify some functions. All team members had backgrounds in HTML and CSS, so some design modifications were made after the initial draft of the site to make it look more visually appealing.

In total we spent roughly 8 people-hours: about 1 hour on brainstorming, about 3 hours on the main coding, and about 4 hours on write-ups, the demo video, hosting setup, and other code modifications. The aspects that took the most time were fine-tuning the visualizations and researching how to make the black hat visualization misleading without being obvious. Late in the process, we also spent time reviewing the code and fact-checking our write-ups against the data, which led to several corrections and the extra credit features.

AI Usage

We used Claude (Anthropic) in three ways. First, we used it to try several ideas for visualizations that suited the data we chose. Most of the manual touch-ups, including the scaling and the other features mentioned above, were done by the team. Secondly, we used it as a reviewer: it read our code and checked our write-ups against the CSV. That review caught some mislabeled outcomes, and an incorrect description for the tooltip behavior. Thirdly, Claude drafted code for the Reveal the Tricks feature and helped outline the demo video script. The team tested and reviewed these changes before using them.
