White Hat

For our white hat visualization, we decided to use the NYPD civilian allegations data set. Specifically, we segmented the data based on allegation category and year, and then calculated the outcome which we labeled as substantiation rate. We also included a marker in the form of a red dot in order to indicate a low sample size. Furthermore, we included a drop down menu in order to switch between substantiation rate and total count of allegations, in order to allow for more clear analysis of the data. We decided to use a color scale based on the number of allegations or percent substantiated, and chose blue as it is typically a color associated with the police. For percent substantiated specifically, we noticed that it was never greater than 50%, so we chose that as the darkest blue, in order to make the other percentage differences more noticeable in terms of color, as it would be harder to tell if the scale went to 100%. We also decided to include a tooltip when a box is hovered over, in order to more clearly explain the data for that specific allegation in that specific year. We made these design choices in order to break down the data as much as possible, and allow viewers to understand the trends over time. We wanted to show how substantiation rates are rising, as well as how the total count of allegations has changed over time, which is why we included a drop down to switch between the two. It allows viewers to understand and extrapolate that although substantiation rates are generally rising, it may be because there are less claims in total, meaning fewer false allegations. In addition, by including the red dots on both the substantiation and total count parts, we allow viewers to understand which pieces of data may be more reliable, and which ones to take with a grain of salt due to a lower sample size.

Black Hat

For our black hat visualization, we once again used the NYPD civilian allegations data set, but this time, we chose to display it in a different manner. We did not segment the data by year, and instead only seperated by allegation, hiding how the number changes over time. In addition, we combined the "exonerated" and "unsubstantiated" categories into one new label - "no misconduct finding" - in order to distort the visualization and mislead the viewer into thinking that the allegations are overwhelmingly dismissed. This is in fact not the case, and many are substantiated, but the officer is exonerated from the charges and does not face punishment. Furthermore, we chose to use red in order to alarm the viewer, and make them think that most misconduct complaints are dismissed, and combined that with a misleading and suggestive title to indicate that the NYPD is a corrupt organization. We also intentionally made the tooltip that is displayed on hover misleading and not specific - it displays "allegations in group" and "total allegations" without stating their actual outcome. In addition, we included percentages on both sides of the bar to highlight how much greater the no misconduct finding side was, and demonstrate that a very small percent of allegations are substantiated. Circling back to how we split the data, we chose to not split by year in order to hide the trends that can be seen in the white hat visualization. By only showing the total percentages, we can disguise the fact that many allegation categories are becoming more substantiated as time goes on, and that the police are becoming more liable for their actions. Instead, by including all the data together, there is no perception of any change over time in behavior or punishment.
